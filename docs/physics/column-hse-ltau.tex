\doctitle{column-hse-ltau: hydrostatic columns on an optical-depth grid}{The physics of \code{run.mode = "column-hse-ltau"}, explained step by step (B.Sc.\ level). HSEMHS.}

\section{The question}
When we observe the Sun we do not see heights; we see light. A Stokes inversion therefore returns the temperature and the
magnetic field not at fixed geometric heights but on surfaces of constant \textbf{optical depth} $\tau_{500}$: at
$\log_{10}\tau_{500}=0,\,-1,\,-2,\dots$ in every pixel. To turn such an atmosphere into a physical one we need, in every
pixel, the gas pressure and the density on each $\tau$ surface, and the \textbf{geometric height} at which that surface
lies. This mode computes them by assuming that every pixel column is in hydrostatic equilibrium on its own.

\section{Optical depth is a measure of mass}
Optical depth is defined by how much light is removed on the way out: going down a distance $dz$ adds
$d\tau=\chi\,dz$ (with $z$ upward, $d\tau=-\chi\,dz$). Write $\chi=\kappa\rho$: the extinction is the product of how much gas
there is and how strongly each kilogram of it absorbs. Then
\[
d\tau=-\kappa\,\rho\,dz=\kappa\,dm,
\]
because $-\rho\,dz=dm$ is the mass added to the column above as we go down by $dz$. So optical depth is \emph{column
mass weighted by the opacity}. For a gas with constant $\kappa$, $\tau=\kappa\,m$ exactly.

\section{Hydrostatic equilibrium in optical depth}
On a height grid, hydrostatic equilibrium reads $dP/dz=-\rho g$ (the weight of a thin slab is carried by the pressure
difference across it). Divide it by $d\tau=-\kappa\rho\,dz$:
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{dP}{d\tau}=\frac{g}{\kappa}\;}
\]
This is the classical form used in stellar atmospheres. Since $dP=g\,dm$ (the pressure is the weight of the gas above),
it simply says that adding the optical depth $d\tau$ means adding the mass $d\tau/\kappa$. With constant $\kappa$ it integrates to
$P=g\tau/\kappa$: \emph{the pressure at optical depth $\tau$ is the weight of the gas that produces that much opacity.}

The levels are equally spaced in $\log_{10}\tau$, so the code integrates in $\ln\tau$ ($d\tau=\tau\,d\ln\tau$) and in
$\ln P$ ($dP=P\,d\ln P$):
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{d\ln P}{d\ln\tau}=\frac{g\,\tau}{\kappa\,P}=\frac{g\,\tau\,\rho}{\chi\,P}\;}
\]
$\rho$ and $\chi$ depend on $T$ and $P$ through the equation of state (ideal gas with ionisation and molecules, from
Wittmann's code, tabulated in $(\ln T,\ln P)$). The equation is therefore integrated numerically, level by level from the
top down.

\section{Why no reference pressure is needed}
On a height grid the pressure needs a prescribed value somewhere (an anchor), because a height coordinate says nothing
about how much gas there is: the same temperature profile is equally hydrostatic at any pressure level. Optical depth is
different: $\tau$ measures the mass above a point, and hydrostatic equilibrium says that the pressure \emph{is} the weight of
that mass. Once $T(\tau)$ and the opacity of the gas are known, the pressure is fixed; there is nothing left to choose.
The flip side is that the result is only as good as the opacity: if the $\tau$ scale of the input was computed with a
different opacity, the pressures differ in proportion.

\section{The top of the column}
The integration needs a starting value at the top level $\tau_1$ (here $10^{-8}$). Above it we assume $\kappa$ stays
constant at its value $\kappa_1$ and that the pressure vanishes at $\tau=0$. Then the equation integrates to
\[
P_1=\frac{g\,\tau_1}{\kappa_1(T_1,P_1)} .
\]
Because $\kappa_1$ itself depends on $P_1$, this is solved by iteration (guess $P_1$, compute $\kappa_1$, update $P_1$,
repeat). An error in this assumption matters only near the top: deeper down the pressure is dominated by the gas added
below $\tau_1$. Roughly, its relative weight is $\tau_1/\tau$, i.e.\ $10^{-8}$ at $\tau=1$.

\section{Heights from the opacity}
A $\tau$ level has a height only once we know how thick each layer is. From $d\tau=-\chi\,dz$:
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{dz}{d\ln\tau}=-\frac{\tau}{\chi}\;}
\]
Where the gas is opaque (large $\chi$), a step in $\ln\tau$ is a thin layer; where it is transparent, the same step is a
thick layer. $z$ is integrated alongside $\ln P$. This fixes the heights in each column only \emph{up to an additive
constant}: we know the distance between the levels, not where the column sits (Sect.~9).

\section{Between grid levels: hot gas over cool gas}
The grid has 10 levels per factor of ten in $\tau$. Between two levels $\ln T$ is assumed to vary linearly with
$\ln\tau$, and the integration takes small steps so that $\ln P$ changes by at most 0.05 per step.

The opacity at 500\,nm comes mainly from the negative hydrogen ion H$^-$, which needs free electrons, and both rise very
steeply with temperature. So cool gas is far more transparent than hot gas. Suppose a hot layer (8000\,K) lies directly on
a cool one (4000\,K) within one grid interval. With the linear interpolation, a whole tenth of a decade in $\tau$ is then
filled with nearly transparent gas. By $dP/d\tau=g/\kappa$, collecting that much optical depth from transparent gas requires a
huge amount of mass, and the pressure jumps by orders of magnitude within the interval. This is a consequence of
representing a sharp temperature change by smooth interpolation on a coarse grid, not of the physics. The code therefore
limits the rise of $\ln P$ to 5 per 0.1\,dex in $\tau$ (\code{cap\_per\_dex} = 50 per dex). This limit is far above the
rises of normal stratifications, so they are untouched, and below a limited interval the pressure returns to the
hydrostatic value within a few levels.

\section{Where the columns sit: total-pressure alignment}
\subsection{Magnetic pressure}
In each column we now know $P$, $\rho$ and the layer thicknesses, but not the height of the column as a whole. The mode
fixes it with a physical argument that involves the magnetic field. The force a magnetic field exerts on the gas, the
Lorentz force, can be rewritten with a vector identity as
\[
\J\times\B=\frac{(\nabla\times\B)\times\B}{\mu_0}=-\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)+\frac{(\B\cdot\nabla)\B}{\mu_0}.
\]
The first term acts like an additional pressure, the \textbf{magnetic pressure} $B^2/2\mu_0$; the second is a tension
along curved field lines.

\subsection{The alignment rule}
Deep in the atmosphere neighbouring columns push on each other until the \emph{total} pressure,
$P+B^2/2\mu_0$, is the same at the same height (if it were not, the higher total pressure would push the neighbour aside).
The mode uses this as its alignment rule: it takes as reference the median, over all columns, of the total pressure on
the deepest $\tau$ level, and shifts every column vertically so that its total pressure reaches the reference at one and
the same height. A column whose deepest level has a total pressure below the reference is continued downward with the
isothermal hydrostatic law $P\propto e^{-z/H}$ until it reaches it.

\subsection{Consequence: the Wilson depression}
A column with a strong field carries part of the load by magnetic pressure, so it needs less gas pressure at the reference
height. Less gas means a lower density, a lower opacity and a longer path to reach $\tau=1$: its $\tau=1$ surface lies
deeper. Sunspots seen near the limb show exactly this, the Wilson depression.

\subsection{The gauge}
Finally all columns are shifted together so that the average height of $\tau=1$ is zero. This is only a choice of origin,
with no physical content.

\section{What the result means}
\begin{itemize}
\item Every column is in hydrostatic equilibrium along $\tau$: the pressure on each $\tau$ level is the weight of the gas
above it.
\item The heights follow from the opacity; the field enters only through the alignment, via its magnetic pressure.
\item The columns are not in horizontal force balance with each other, and the tension of the field is ignored. Adding
both is the purpose of \code{mhs-poisson-ltau}, which starts from this result.
\end{itemize}
