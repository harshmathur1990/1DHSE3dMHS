\section{What the mode does}
Stokes inversions return the atmosphere on surfaces of constant optical depth, $\log_{10}\tau_{500}=$ const, not at fixed
geometric heights. For such an atmosphere, $T(x,y,\tau)$ and $\B(x,y,\tau)$, this mode computes in every column the
gas pressure $P(\tau)$, the density $\rho(\tau)$ and the \textbf{geometric height} $z(\tau)$ that hydrostatic
equilibrium implies. As in \code{column-hse-z}, each column is independent; the only coupling between columns is the
choice of their vertical offsets (Sect.~5). It is the starting state of \code{mhs-poisson-ltau}.

\section{The equations}
Hydrostatic equilibrium $dP/dz=-\rho g$ and the definition of optical depth $d\tau=-\chi\,dz$ combine into equations
along $\ln\tau$:
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{d\ln P}{d\ln\tau}=\frac{g\,\tau\,\rho}{\chi\,P}=\frac{g\,\tau}{\kappa\,P},\qquad
\frac{dz}{d\ln\tau}=-\frac{\tau}{\chi}\;}
\]
The first is the classical stellar-atmosphere form $dP/d\tau=g/\kappa$: the pressure at a given optical depth is the
weight of the column above it, $P\approx g\,\tau/\bar\kappa$. The second turns the optical-depth scale into a height
scale. Both right-hand sides depend on $P$ through the EOS, so they are integrated together, down from the top level.

\textbf{Top boundary.} Above the top level $\kappa$ is taken as constant, so $P_{\rm top}=g\,\tau_{\rm top}/\kappa(T_{\rm top},P_{\rm top})$,
solved as a fixed point in $P_{\rm top}$. Since the top level is at $\tau=10^{-8}$, an error in this assumption changes
$P$ only near the top: it decays as $\tau$ grows and the integrated weight dominates.

\textbf{No anchor is needed.} Unlike the height grid, the pressure scale is fixed by $\tau$ itself (the mass above a
given $\tau$), so no reference pressure is supplied. What $\tau$ does \emph{not} fix is the absolute height of a column:
$z$ is known only up to a constant per column (Sect.~5).

\section{Between grid levels: hot gas over cool gas}
Inside each 0.1\,dex interval $\ln T$ is taken linear in $\log_{10}\tau$, and the equations are integrated with adaptive
second-order (Heun) substeps that change $\ln P$ by at most 0.05. Where a hot layer lies directly on top of a much
cooler one (for example 8200\,K over 4200\,K within 0.1\,dex), the opacity of the cool gas is orders of magnitude lower.
The linear interpolation then puts a whole 0.1\,dex of nearly transparent gas below the hot layer, and $dP/d\tau=g/\kappa$
makes the pressure jump: in about 1\,\% of the columns $\ln P$ would rise by $\sim$20 within one interval
($P\to6\times10^8$\,Pa) and push $\tau=1$ a megametre down. This is an artefact of representing a sharp temperature jump on a
coarse $\tau$ grid, not physics. The rise of $\ln P$ is therefore capped at \code{cap\_per\_dex} = 50 per dex (5 per 0.1\,dex),
which still allows the physical rises (up to 5 per 0.1\,dex in 99\,\% of the columns). Below a capped interval the pressure
returns to $g\tau/\kappa$ within a few levels. On the production cube 2585 of the $\sim2.4\times10^7$ intervals are capped.

\section{Aligning the columns}
Each column's height scale has an arbitrary zero. The mode chooses the offsets so that the \textbf{total pressure}
$P+B^2/2\mu_0$ is equal in all columns at one height: the global median of the total pressure on the bottom $\tau$
level. The idea is lateral pressure balance at depth: deep down, flux tubes and their surroundings must push equally
hard on each other, so a strong-field column, whose magnetic pressure carries part of the load, has a lower gas
pressure at the same height and sits deeper (Wilson depression). Where a column's bottom total pressure is below the
reference, it is extrapolated hydrostatically below its last level (8192 of 16384 columns in a test crop; by
construction about half). With \code{align = "none"} every column keeps $z=0$ at $\tau=1$.

Finally all heights are shifted together so that the mean height of $\tau=1$ is zero (a global gauge; it has no
physical content).

\section{What the result means}
\begin{itemize}
\item Every column is hydrostatic in $\tau$. The pressure on each $\tau$ level is essentially the weight of the gas above,
independent of the neighbours. Horizontal force balance is not imposed.
\item The heights come from the opacity: where the gas is opaque, a $\Delta\log\tau$ step is geometrically thin, where it
is transparent the step is thick. Hot granules (high H$^-$ opacity) therefore have their $\tau=1$ surface high, cool
lanes and strong-field columns (evacuated by magnetic pressure) low.
\item Production cube (\code{IO/input-mhs-ltau.nc}, $504\times504\times95$ levels): $P(\tau=1)$ median 13.2\,kPa;
$z(\tau=1)$ percentiles 1/50/99 $=-91$/$-41$/$+293$\,km before the gauge; the $+293$\,km tail is hot granules, strong-field columns
are depressed. Levels near $\tau=10^{-8}$ lie at tens of Mm in the hot columns, as hydrostatics requires.
\item The magnetic field enters only through the alignment (magnetic pressure). Its tension and the horizontal force
balance between columns are the subject of \code{mhs-poisson-ltau}.
\end{itemize}
