\section{The question}
We are given a snapshot of the solar atmosphere: the temperature $T$ and the magnetic field $\Bo$ at every point of a box.
In the real Sun (or in the simulation the snapshot comes from) the gas moves, so the forces on it do not cancel: the net
force is what accelerates it. We want a \textbf{static} atmosphere, with zero net force everywhere, that looks as much as
possible like the snapshot: the temperature exactly as given, the magnetic field as close as possible to $\Bo$, and the
pressure (hence the density) adjusted. Such a state is called \textbf{magnetohydrostatic (MHS)} equilibrium. Unlike the
\code{mhs-poisson} modes, which change only the pressure, this mode also changes the field. That is what makes a true balance
possible, as Sect.~4 explains.

\section{The equations of magnetohydrostatics}
Three forces per unit volume act on a small volume of gas: the pressure force $-\nabla P$, gravity $-\rho g\zh$ and the
Lorentz force $\J\times\B$ of the field on the currents in the ionised gas, with $\J=\nabla\times\B/\mu_0$ (Amp\`ere's law).
The gas is static if
\[
\boxed{\;-\nabla P+\J\times\B-\rho g\,\zh=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\B=0\;}
\]
The second equation (no magnetic monopoles) holds for every real field. A vector identity splits the Lorentz force into
\[
\J\times\B=-\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)+\frac{(\B\cdot\nabla)\B}{\mu_0}:
\]
a \textbf{magnetic pressure} $B^2/2\mu_0$ (field lines push apart) and a \textbf{magnetic tension} (curved field lines pull
like stretched rubber bands). The ratio $\beta=2\mu_0P/B^2$ of gas to magnetic pressure decides which force dominates: deep
down $\beta\gg1$, high up $\beta\ll1$. Where $\beta\ll1$, a static state needs a nearly \textbf{force-free} field,
$\J\times\B\approx0$, with currents flowing along the field lines.

Without a field, the vertical equation is hydrostatic equilibrium $dP/dz=-\rho g$. With the ideal gas law
$\rho=\bar\mu m_{\rm H}P/(k_BT)$ ($\bar\mu$ the mean particle mass in units of the hydrogen mass, which changes with
ionisation; it comes from a tabulated equation of state) this becomes $d\ln P/dz=-1/H$ with the scale height
$H=k_BT/(\bar\mu m_{\rm H}g)$, about 140\,km in the photosphere.

\section{Why the field has to change}
Take the curl of the force balance. The curl of a gradient vanishes and $\nabla\times(\rho g\zh)=g(\partial_y\rho,-\partial_x\rho,0)$,
so a static state requires
\[
\nabla\times(\J\times\B)=g\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y},\,-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x},\,0\right).
\]
Wherever hot and cool gas lie side by side at the same height (and in the Sun they do everywhere: hot rising granules next
to cool sinking lanes), the density varies horizontally, and only the magnetic tension can hold that up. A field taken from a
moving snapshot was never shaped for this, so with $\B=\Bo$ no pressure field can be static (the \code{mhs-poisson} modes
show how far a pressure change alone gets). Here we let $\B$ change as well, \emph{as little as possible}. That turns the
problem into a compromise between ``balance the forces'' and ``stay close to $\Bo$''.

\section{Making $\nabla\cdot\B=0$ automatic: the vector potential}
Instead of the field itself, the unknown is the vector potential $\mathbf A$, with $\B=\nabla\times\mathbf A+\bar b\,\zh$.
The divergence of a curl is always zero, so $\nabla\cdot\B=0$ holds automatically and never has to be imposed. $\bar b$
is needed because the box is periodic: the curl of a periodic potential has zero average vertical component over every
horizontal plane, so the net vertical magnetic flux through the box has to be added separately.

\section{The staggered grid}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
The quantities live at different places in each grid cell:
\begin{itemize}
\item $P$, $\rho$, $T$ at the cell centre;
\item each field component on the cell face it points through ($B_x$ on the faces perpendicular to $x$, \dots); $B_x$ there is
the magnetic flux through the face divided by its area;
\item $\mathbf A$ and the currents $\J$ on the cell edges.
\end{itemize}
The flux out of a cell is the sum over its six faces. For $\B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ each face flux is a sum of $\mathbf A$
around the face's edges (Stokes' theorem), and every edge appears in two faces of the cell with opposite signs, so the total
is \emph{exactly} zero: $\nabla\cdot\B=0$ holds on the grid itself, not just approximately.
\end{minipage}\hfill

\smallskip
The field components and currents are differences of neighbouring values, e.g.
$B_z=\partial A_y/\partial x-\partial A_x/\partial y+\bar b$ and $J_y=(\partial B_x/\partial z-\partial B_z/\partial x)/\mu_0$:
a current is a \emph{change} of the field across space, so a uniform field carries none. Each component of the force balance
is evaluated on the faces of its own direction:
\[
R_x=\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}-(J_yB_z-J_zB_y),\quad
R_y=\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}-(J_zB_x-J_xB_z),\quad
R_z=\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}+\rho g-(J_xB_y-J_yB_x).
\]
Only the two \emph{other} field components enter each one: a field along $x$ exerts no force along $x$.

\section{The quantity that is minimised}
\[
\Phi=\underbrace{\lambda_F\Big\langle\frac{|\R|^2}{S^2}\Big\rangle}_{\Phi_F}
+\underbrace{\Big\langle\frac{(\B-\Bo)^2}{\sigma^2}\Big\rangle}_{\Phi_B}
+\underbrace{\lambda_S\Big\langle\Big|\frac{h^2\,\partial^2(\B-\Bo)}{\sigma}\Big|^2\Big\rangle}_{\Phi_S}
+\underbrace{\lambda_F\lambda_Q\Big\langle\Big|\frac{B_{\rm ref}\,\delta^2\J}{S}\Big|^2\Big\rangle}_{\Phi_Q}
\]
$\langle\cdot\rangle$ is a volume-weighted average over the grid. Each term, and why it is there:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\Phi_F$, force balance.} The residual is divided by the local weight of the gas $S\approx\rho g$ (from the
starting state, with a floor so it never becomes tiny), so $|\R|/S=1$ means an unbalanced force as large as gravity. Without
this scaling the deep, dense layers would dominate everything.
\item \textbf{$\Phi_B$, closeness to the given field.} Each face compares its own component with $\Bo$ interpolated to that
face, in units of $\sigma$. If the deviations were random errors of size $\sigma$, $\Phi_B$ would be about 1 (this is the
familiar $\chi^2$ per data point). With a uniform $\sigma$ of 5\,G, any change of the field costs the same everywhere; there
is deliberately no relative tolerance, because for observations the measurement noise of the Stokes profiles does not
translate into a meaningful field error.
\item \textbf{$\Phi_S$, smoothness of the change.} The second differences ($h^2\partial^2$, with $h$ the grid spacing) of
$\B-\Bo$ are penalised: the model may differ from $\Bo$ by a smooth, large-scale correction, but not by cell-to-cell noise.
\item \textbf{$\Phi_Q$, no grid-scale currents.} Second differences of the currents along the grid are penalised. Without it
the solver could balance forces with currents that flip sign from one cell to the next, which are numerical artefacts, not
physics.
\end{itemize}
The weights $\lambda$ set the trade-off. A larger $\lambda_F$ favours force balance at the price of a larger change of the
field; a smaller one keeps the field closer to $\Bo$ and leaves more force imbalance. There is no unique ``right'' value; the
defaults are recorded with every run.

\section{Boundary conditions and the pressure level}
The box is periodic in $x$ and $y$: what leaves one side enters the other. At the top and bottom the horizontal force
equations are not used on the outermost layers (they lack the vertical derivative of the field there). The force balance
fixes pressure \emph{differences}; the overall pressure level is set by requiring the mean of $\ln P$ on the bottom plane
to equal $\ln P_{\rm ref}$, a single number, not a pressure map. Two safeguards keep the pressure physical: in one step $\ln P$
may change by at most 5 anywhere, and $P$ may never fall below $e^{-3}$ times its starting value.

\section{How the minimum is found}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Starting pressure.} Every column is put in exact hydrostatic equilibrium on the grid (field ignored),
upward from $P_{\rm ref}$ at the bottom.
\item \textbf{Starting field.} With the pressure frozen, $\mathbf A$ and $\bar b$ are chosen so that $\nabla\times\mathbf A+\bar b\zh$
matches $\Bo$ as closely as possible ($\Phi_B+\Phi_S$ only). This is a linear least-squares problem.
\item \textbf{Joint steps.} The force residual depends non-linearly on the unknowns ($\J\times\B$ is quadratic in $\B$,
$\rho$ depends on $P$). Each step replaces it by its linear approximation in the changes of $\ln P$, $\mathbf A$ and
$\bar b$ (Gauss--Newton, the least-squares form of Newton's method), solves for the change that minimises the
approximated $\Phi$, and accepts it only if the true $\Phi$ decreases. If it does not, the step is halved along the same
direction (a backtracking line search) until it does.
\item \textbf{Stop} when $\Phi$ has stopped changing: its relative change is below $10^{-6}$ on three accepted steps in a
row.
\end{enumerate}
Many different potentials give the same field (adding a gradient to $\mathbf A$ does not change $\nabla\times\mathbf A$).
Such changes have no effect on $\Phi$; a tiny damping term keeps the linear systems well defined, and all results are judged
by the physical quantities $P$ and $\B$.

\section{The visible surface}
After every accepted step the optical depth at 500\,nm, $\tau_{500}(z)=\tau_{\rm top}+\int_z^{z_{\rm top}}\chi_{500}\,dz'$,
is recomputed in every column from the current pressure, and with it the height of $\tau_{500}=1$, the visible surface. The
opacity $\chi_{500}$ depends only on $T$ and $P$, so the field changes the visible surface only through the pressure it
demands. A strong flux tube is partly supported by its magnetic pressure, so its gas pressure is lower, its gas is more
transparent, and its $\tau=1$ surface lies deeper: the Wilson depression.

\section{Summary}
The mode looks for the static atmosphere closest to the snapshot. The temperature is kept exactly, $\nabla\cdot\B=0$ holds
exactly through the vector potential on a staggered grid, and the pressure and field are adjusted together to minimise a
weighted sum of the force imbalance, the change of the field, and penalties against grid-scale noise. Unlike the
pressure-only modes, it can in principle remove the buoyancy part of the imbalance, because only a changed field can supply
the magnetic tension that holds up horizontal density differences.
