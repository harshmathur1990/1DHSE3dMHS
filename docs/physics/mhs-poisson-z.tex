\doctitle{mhs-poisson-z: a pressure-only magnetohydrostatic correction on a geometric-height grid}{Physics and method of \code{run.mode = "mhs-poisson-z"} in HSEMHS. A self-contained description.}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Starting from an atmosphere whose columns are each in hydrostatic equilibrium, this mode adjusts the gas pressure
in three dimensions so that pressure, gravity and the Lorentz force of a given magnetic field balance each other as well as
possible. Temperature and magnetic field stay fixed; only the pressure changes. Because a fixed field generally cannot hold
up every horizontal density contrast, an exact equilibrium does not exist, and the pressure is found as the minimiser of a
weighted sum of squared relative force residuals. We derive the force balance, show why its pressure part is a Poisson
problem, explain what a pressure change can and cannot achieve, define the residuals and their weights, and describe the
Gauss--Newton iteration, the Fourier-mode preconditioner, the step control, the stopping rule, the treatment of the
low-$\beta$ upper atmosphere and the computation of the optical depth. All assumptions, including how the pressure level is
fixed, are listed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A hydrostatic atmosphere in which every vertical column carries its own weight separately has two shortcomings. First,
neighbouring columns of different temperature have different pressures at the same height, so horizontal pressure forces
remain unbalanced. Second, the magnetic field is ignored. This mode keeps the temperature $T$ and the magnetic field $\B$
exactly as given and asks: \emph{which pressure field balances, as well as possible, the pressure force, gravity and the
magnetic force in all three directions?} The idea comes from the FIRTEZ-dz inversion code (Borrero et al.), where the
atmosphere changes in every inversion cycle and a pressure-only correction restores the force balance far faster than a
correction of the field would.

\textbf{Input:} $T$ and $\B$ on a three-dimensional geometric-height grid that is periodic in $x$ and $y$; the gravity $g$;
a reference pressure $P_{\rm ref}$ for the starting state; the height $z_{\rm cut}$ below which the force balance is solved.
\textbf{Output:} $P$, $\rho$ and $\tau_{500}$ on the grid, the height of $\tau_{500}=1$ in every column, and the starting
(column-hydrostatic) pressure for comparison.

\section{The force balance}\label{sec:force}
\subsection{The three forces}
A small volume of gas feels three forces per unit volume:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the \textbf{pressure force} $-\nabla P$, pushing gas from high to low pressure;
\item \textbf{gravity} $-\rho g\,\zh$;
\item the \textbf{Lorentz force} $\Lv=\J\times\B$ of the magnetic field on the electric currents in the ionised gas. In a slowly
changing plasma the currents follow from the field by Amp\`ere's law, $\J=\nabla\times\B/\mu_0$.
\end{enumerate}
With a vector identity the Lorentz force splits into two parts with a clear meaning:
\begin{equation}
\J\times\B=\frac{(\nabla\times\B)\times\B}{\mu_0}=\underbrace{-\nabla\!\left(\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)}_{\text{magnetic pressure}}
+\underbrace{\frac{(\B\cdot\nabla)\B}{\mu_0}}_{\text{magnetic tension}} .\label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
Field lines push sideways on each other like a gas with pressure $B^2/2\mu_0$, and curved field lines pull like stretched
rubber bands.

\subsection{Magnetohydrostatic equilibrium in three components}
The gas is static when the three forces cancel:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\F\equiv-\nabla P+\J\times\B-\rho\,g\,\zh=0\;}\label{eq:mhs}
\end{equation}
or, component by component,
\begin{equation}
-\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}+L_x=0,\qquad -\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}+L_y=0,\qquad -\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}-\rho g+L_z=0 .
\label{eq:mhs3}
\end{equation}
In this mode $\Lv$ is a \emph{given} force field, computed once from the fixed $\B$, and $\rho$ depends on $P$ through the
equation of state. The question is therefore which single scalar field $P(x,y,z)$ balances gravity plus a given vector
force field.

\subsection{Why it is called a Poisson step}
Take the divergence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mhs}, using $\nabla\cdot\nabla P=\nabla^2P$:
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2P=\nabla\cdot(\J\times\B)-g\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial z}.\label{eq:poisson}
\end{equation}
This is a \textbf{Poisson equation} for $P$, the same type of equation as that for the electric potential of a charge
distribution, with a right-hand side that itself depends on $P$ through $\rho$. FIRTEZ solves essentially this equation,
iterating on the density. Poisson problems in a horizontally periodic box can be solved very fast with Fourier transforms
(Sect.~\ref{sec:prec}). The method used here does not solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson} literally but minimises the residual of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mhs} (Sect.~\ref{sec:ls}); each of its linear steps turns out to be a Poisson-type equation.

\section{What a pressure correction can do, and what it cannot}\label{sec:helm}
\subsection{Gradient and non-gradient forces}
A force field in the box can be split (the \textbf{Helmholtz decomposition}) into
\begin{equation}
\F=\underbrace{-\nabla\phi}_{\text{gradient part}}+\underbrace{\mathbf S}_{\text{non-gradient part}},
\qquad \int\mathbf S\cdot\nabla\psi\,dV=0\ \text{for every function }\psi .
\end{equation}
The gradient part is the slope of a scalar potential $\phi$, like a pressure force, and has no curl. The non-gradient part
carries the whole curl of $\F$, and the orthogonality condition means, after integration by parts, that it has no divergence.
The two parts are orthogonal, so their sizes add like the sides of a right triangle,
$\int|\F|^2dV=\int|\nabla\phi|^2dV+\int|\mathbf S|^2dV$. The split is unique once boundary conditions are fixed: the box is
periodic in $x$ and $y$ but has a top and a bottom, where conditions (and an additive constant in $\phi$) have to be
specified.

A change of pressure $\delta P$ changes the force by $-\nabla\delta P$, a pure gradient. If the density did not change, a
pressure correction could remove the gradient part ($\delta P=-\phi$) and could never touch $\mathbf S$. This picture explains
what the method can achieve; the solver itself does not compute such a projection. It minimises a weighted sum of squares
of the relative residual (Sect.~\ref{sec:ls}), a non-linear problem, so it neither removes the gradient part exactly nor
leaves $\mathbf S$ untouched.

\subsection{Which forces are not gradients?}
Take the curl of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mhs}. The curl of a gradient is zero, and
$\nabla\times(\rho g\zh)=g\,(\partial_y\rho,\,-\partial_x\rho,\,0)$, so
\begin{equation}
\nabla\times\F=\nabla\times(\J\times\B)-g\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y},\,-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x},\,0\right).
\end{equation}
Term by term: the \textbf{pressure} force is always a gradient. \textbf{Gravity} is a gradient only if $\rho$ is uniform on
each horizontal plane; a heavy column next to a light one at the same height cannot be static (\textbf{buoyancy}). The
\textbf{Lorentz force} has a gradient part (the magnetic pressure) and in general a non-gradient part (the tension). Static
equilibrium therefore requires the \textbf{curl condition}
\begin{equation}
\nabla\times(\J\times\B)=g\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y},\,-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x},\,0\right):
\label{eq:curl}
\end{equation}
only the tension of the field can hold up horizontal density differences. Without a field this forces
$\partial_x\rho=\partial_y\rho=0$. With a given $\B$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:curl} holds only if the field happens to have exactly the
right tension everywhere. There is no reason to expect this of a field taken from a moving simulation or an inversion, so in
general \emph{no pressure makes the atmosphere exactly static}, and the mode looks for the best compromise.

\subsection{The density loophole}
The density is not fixed: at fixed $T$ a pressure change changes it,
\begin{equation}
\delta\rho=\rho\left.\frac{\partial\ln\rho}{\partial\ln P}\right|_T\frac{\delta P}{P},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad \delta(\nabla\times\F)=-g\,\nabla\times(\delta\rho\,\zh).
\end{equation}
By making the pressure fall faster with height under a dense column, the correction lowers that column's density at a given
height and reduces the horizontal density contrast, and with it part of $\mathbf S$. What it can never remove is the part
caused by temperature differences at fixed pressure or by the tension of $\B$: that requires changing $\B$ or $T$.

\section{The quantity minimised}\label{sec:ls}
\subsection{Relative residual}
Divide the force by the pressure and use $u=\ln P$ ($\nabla P/P=\nabla u$, $\rho g/P=g\,q=1/H$):
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\R\equiv\frac{\F}{P}=-\nabla u-g\,q(T,u)\,\zh+\Lv\,e^{-u}\;}\label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P=e^u$ stays positive across the many decades of pressure in the box.
\item $\R$ is a \emph{relative} imbalance. Minimising $|\F|$ itself would reward making the pressure small everywhere (all
forces scale with $P$); dividing by $P$ removes that false incentive.
\item Without the Lorentz force, $R_z=0$ is $du/dz=-1/H$, ordinary hydrostatic equilibrium.
\item $|\R|\,H\approx|\F|/(\rho g)$: multiplied by the scale height, the residual is the unbalanced force in units of the
weight of the gas. A value of 1 means an unbalanced force as large as gravity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{More equations than unknowns: least squares}
At every point the force balance gives three equations, but there is only one unknown, the pressure. With more equations
than unknowns and, by Sect.~\ref{sec:helm}, no exact solution, we look for the \textbf{best compromise}: the pressure that
minimises the weighted sum of squared residuals, just as fitting a straight line through many points minimises the squared
distances. The objective is a discrete volume integral,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\Phi(u)=\sum_{k,j,i}w_k\,\bar H_k^2\left[(R_x)_{ijk}^2+(R_y)_{ijk}^2\right]+\sum_{k,j,i}h_k\,\bar H_{k+1/2}^2\,(R_z)_{ij,k+1/2}^2\;}
\label{eq:Phi}
\end{equation}
(the discrete residuals are defined in Sect.~\ref{sec:disc}). $w_k$ and $h_k$ are the thicknesses of the cells around the
levels and between them, so $\Phi$ approximates $\int|\R|^2\bar H^2\,dz$ summed over the columns. $\bar H_k$ is the median of
$H$ over layer $k$ in the starting state and stays fixed during the solve; $\bar H_{k+1/2}=\sqrt{\bar H_k\bar H_{k+1}}$. The
factor $\bar H^2$ makes each term a force in units of the local weight, so that the deep, dense layers, whose forces are
orders of magnitude larger, do not swamp the upper ones. The reported measure of the imbalance is
${\rm RMS}\,|\R|\bar H=\sqrt{\Phi/W}$, with $W$ the sum of all weights ($w_k$ twice, for $x$ and $y$, and $h_k$) over all
columns.

\subsection{The pressure level: the reference pressure}\label{sec:level}
Force balance hardly fixes the overall level of the pressure. For an ideal gas of fixed ionisation, multiplying $P$
everywhere by a constant leaves $\nabla u$ and $q$ unchanged, so only the Lorentz term $\Lv e^{-u}$ and the weak pressure
dependence of the ionisation would notice. The level is therefore fixed separately, in two stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \textbf{starting state} is the column-hydrostatic atmosphere with the same pressure $P_{\rm ref}$ at the bottom of
every column (for the production input, the median of the simulation's own pressure over the bottom plane). It is computed
by integrating $du/dz=-g\,q$ upward from the bottom in every column (classical Runge--Kutta with substep doubling to a
tolerance of $10^{-6}$ in $u$). The bottom is used because there, deep below the visible surface, the true pressure varies
least across a horizontal plane.
\item During the solve each step is penalised for changing the \textbf{mean of $u$ over the bottom plane}
(Sect.~\ref{sec:normal}). The pattern of $u$ on the bottom plane is free to change, so the horizontal forces there can be
balanced; only its mean is held near the starting value $\ln P_{\rm ref}$. This is a penalty, not an exact constraint, and the
limit on the size of each step can shift it slightly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\texorpdfstring{The low-$\beta$ problem and the cut at $z_{\rm cut}$}{The low-beta problem and the cut at z\_cut}}\label{sec:cut}
The plasma beta $\beta=2\mu_0P/B^2$ compares gas and magnetic pressure. Deep down $\beta\gg1$: the gas dominates. Higher up
$\beta\ll1$: the Lorentz force dwarfs every gas force, and a static state needs a nearly \textbf{force-free} field,
$\J\times\B\approx0$ (currents along the field lines). A field from a dynamic simulation or an inversion is not force-free.
With $\B$ fixed, the only way to balance a large Lorentz force is a large pressure gradient, i.e.\ a large pressure; and
because $\partial P/\partial z$ links each layer to the one below, raising the pressure high up raises it in the photosphere
too, which would destroy the result where it matters most.

The force balance is therefore solved only on the levels with $z_k\le z_{\rm cut}$ (default 1\,Mm), where the gas still
dominates. Above $z_{\rm cut}$ each column is integrated in plain hydrostatic equilibrium, $du/dz=-g\,q$, upward from the
solved pressure at the first level below the cut (Sect.~\ref{sec:above}). FIRTEZ makes the same physical choice more
gradually (its magnetic term decays exponentially above a chosen height). $z_{\rm cut}$ is a physical parameter: it marks
where we stop trusting the given field to be in equilibrium.

\section{Assumptions}\label{sec:assume}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{A\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.2em]
\item \textbf{Static gas:} velocities neglected.
\item \textbf{Fixed temperature and field.} $T$ and $\B$ are not changed; hence $\J$ and $\Lv$ are fixed, computed once.
\item \textbf{Best compromise, not equilibrium:} the solution minimises Eq.~\eqref{eq:Phi}; an exact equilibrium generally does
not exist (Sect.~\ref{sec:helm}).
\item \textbf{Weights} $\bar H_k$ from the starting state, fixed during the solve.
\item \textbf{Pressure level:} equal $P_{\rm ref}$ at the bottom of every column in the starting state; afterwards the mean
of $\ln P$ over the bottom plane held by a per-step penalty (Sect.~\ref{sec:level}).
\item \textbf{Horizontally periodic box}; no condition is imposed at the top and bottom of the solved region other than those
implied by the residuals there (the natural boundary conditions of the least-squares problem).
\item \textbf{Hydrostatic above $z_{\rm cut}$:} no Lorentz force and no horizontal balance there.
\item \textbf{LTE ideal gas} with ionisation and molecules from the table; \textbf{constant vertical gravity}, plane-parallel
geometry.
\item \textbf{Discretisation:} field and pressure at the same grid points; second-order differences.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The discrete problem}\label{sec:disc}
\subsection{Grid of the solved region}
The solved region consists of the levels $k_c\dots N_z$ ($k_c$ the first level with $z_k\le z_{\rm cut}$), renumbered
$k=1\dots n$ from the top. Every unknown $u_{ijk}$ sits at a grid point. Between levels $k$ and $k+1$ the spacing is
$h_k=z_k-z_{k+1}$ and the face lies at $z_{k+1/2}=\tfrac12(z_k+z_{k+1})$; the outer faces lie half a spacing outside the
end levels. The cell thickness is $w_k=z_{k-1/2}-z_{k+1/2}$. Altogether there are $nN_xN_y$ unknowns and
$(3n-1)N_xN_y$ residuals.

\subsection{The Lorentz force}
The current is computed on the full grid with second-order differences, centred and periodic in $x$ and $y$,
\[
(\partial_xf)_{ijk}=\frac{f_{i+1,jk}-f_{i-1,jk}}{2\Delta x},\qquad (\partial_yf)_{ijk}=\frac{f_{i,j+1,k}-f_{i,j-1,k}}{2\Delta y},
\]
and in $z$ with the second-order three-point formula for unequal spacing (one-sided at the top and bottom levels). Then
\begin{equation}
\J=\frac1{\mu_0}\big(\partial_yB_z-\partial_zB_y,\ \partial_zB_x-\partial_xB_z,\ \partial_xB_y-\partial_yB_x\big),\qquad
\Lv=\big(J_yB_z-J_zB_y,\ J_zB_x-J_xB_z,\ J_xB_y-J_yB_x\big).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The residuals}
The horizontal residuals live at the grid points,
\begin{equation}
(R_x)_{ijk}=-\frac{u_{i+1,jk}-u_{i-1,jk}}{2\Delta x}+(L_x)_{ijk}\,e^{-u_{ijk}},\qquad
(R_y)_{ijk}=-\frac{u_{i,j+1,k}-u_{i,j-1,k}}{2\Delta y}+(L_y)_{ijk}\,e^{-u_{ijk}},\label{eq:Rxy}
\end{equation}
and the vertical one on the interior faces $k+1/2$, $k=1\dots n-1$:
\begin{equation}
(R_z)_{ij,k+1/2}=-\frac{u_{ijk}-u_{ij,k+1}}{h_k}-\frac{g}{2}\big(q_{ijk}+q_{ij,k+1}\big)
+\frac12\big((L_z)_{ijk}e^{-u_{ijk}}+(L_z)_{ij,k+1}e^{-u_{ij,k+1}}\big),\label{eq:Rz}
\end{equation}
with $q_{ijk}=\rho(T_{ijk},e^{u_{ijk}})\,e^{-u_{ijk}}$. Placing $R_z$ between the levels makes the vertical pressure
difference a compact two-point difference; the density and the Lorentz force are averaged to the face.

\section{The Gauss--Newton iteration}\label{sec:gn}
\subsection{Linearisation}
$\R$ depends on $u$ linearly through $-\nabla u$, but non-linearly through $q(T,u)$ and the factor $e^{-u}$. Newton's method
for one equation $f(u)=0$ replaces $f$ by its tangent and solves $f(u)+f'(u)\,\delta u=0$. For a least-squares problem the
analogue is \textbf{Gauss--Newton}: replace the residual vector by its linear approximation in the step,
\begin{equation}
\R(u+\delta u)\approx\R(u)+\mathsf J\,\delta u ,\qquad \mathsf J=\frac{\partial\R}{\partial u},
\end{equation}
and minimise the resulting quadratic $\Phi(u+\delta u)\approx(\R+\mathsf J\delta u)^{\!\top}\mathsf W(\R+\mathsf J\delta u)$.
The derivatives are
\begin{align}
\delta(R_x)_{ijk}&=-\frac{\delta u_{i+1,jk}-\delta u_{i-1,jk}}{2\Delta x}-(L_x)_{ijk}e^{-u_{ijk}}\,\delta u_{ijk}
\quad\text{(and likewise for $y$)},\\
\delta(R_z)_{ij,k+1/2}&=-\big(a_k\,\delta u_{ijk}+b_k\,\delta u_{ij,k+1}\big),\\
a_k&=\frac1{h_k}+\frac12\Big(g\,q'_{ijk}+(L_z)_{ijk}e^{-u_{ijk}}\Big),\qquad
b_k=-\frac1{h_k}+\frac12\Big(g\,q'_{ij,k+1}+(L_z)_{ij,k+1}e^{-u_{ij,k+1}}\Big),
\end{align}
with $q'=\partial q/\partial u=q\,\big(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln P|_T-1\big)$, taken from the derivative of the table
interpolation. ($a_k$ and $b_k$ also depend on $i$, $j$; the indices are dropped for brevity.)

\subsection{The normal equations}\label{sec:normal}
Setting the derivative of the quadratic with respect to $\delta u$ to zero gives the \textbf{normal equations}
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\big(\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\mathsf J+\mathsf E+\mathsf A\big)\,\delta u=-\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\,\R\;}
\label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
with two additional terms:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathsf E$, a small diagonal damping, $E_k=10^{-2}\,\kappa_1^2\,w_k\bar H_k^2$ with $\kappa_1=2\pi/(N_x\Delta x)$ the
smallest horizontal wavenumber of the box: one per cent of the horizontal stiffness of the longest wave. It keeps the
system well posed for components that the residuals barely see.
\item $\mathsf A$, the \textbf{anchor} penalty of Sect.~\ref{sec:level}: the term $\tfrac12A\big(\sum_{\rm bottom}\delta
u\big)^2$ is added to the quadratic, so $\mathsf A=A\,\mathbf c\mathbf c^{\!\top}$ with $\mathbf c$ the indicator vector of
the bottom-plane points and $A=w_{\rm a}\,\bar H_{n-1/2}^2/(h_{n-1}N_xN_y)$ ($w_{\rm a}$ = \code{anchor\_w}, default 1),
comparable to the vertical stiffness of the lowest face.
\end{itemize}
The right-hand side $-\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\R$ is minus half the gradient of $\Phi$. Since $\mathsf J$ contains the
difference operators $-\nabla$, the leading part of $\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\mathsf J$ is
$-\nabla\cdot\big(w\bar H^2\nabla\,\cdot\big)$, a weighted Laplacian: \emph{each step is a Poisson equation for $\delta u$},
plus local terms from the equation of state and the Lorentz force. Gauss--Newton neglects the second derivatives of the
residuals. That is accurate where the residuals are small, but when a sizeable residual remains at the minimum, as here,
the convergence is only linear: $\Phi$ decreases by a roughly constant factor per step rather than ever faster.

\subsection{Solving the normal equations: preconditioned conjugate gradients}
Equation~\eqref{eq:normal} is a symmetric, positive-definite linear system with as many unknowns as solved grid points (tens
of millions). It is never written out as a matrix. The \textbf{conjugate-gradient} (CG) method needs only products of the
matrix with a vector, and each product applies $\mathsf J$ and $\mathsf J^{\!\top}$ with the stencils above. CG starts from
$\delta u=0$ and builds the solution from successive search directions, each conjugate to the previous ones. It converges in
few iterations if a good \textbf{preconditioner} $\mathsf M\approx\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\mathsf J+\mathsf E+\mathsf A$
with a cheap inverse is available: CG is then applied in effect to $\mathsf M^{-1}(\mathsf J^{\!\top}\mathsf W\mathsf
J+\mathsf E+\mathsf A)$, which is close to the identity. CG stops when the residual of the linear system has fallen to
$10^{-3}$ of its starting value (\code{cg\_tol}) or after 80 iterations (\code{cg\_maxit}). An inexact solution is
acceptable, because the step is in any case only the minimiser of a linear model.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The preconditioner: Fourier modes in $x$ and $y$}{The preconditioner: Fourier modes in x and y}}\label{sec:prec}
In a horizontally periodic box any function can be written as a sum of waves,
$\delta u_{ijk}=\sum_{k_x,k_y}\hat u_k(k_x,k_y)\,e^{\,i(k_xx_i+k_yy_j)}$. A centred difference acts on a wave as a
multiplication, $\partial_x\to i\sin(k_x\Delta x)/\Delta x$, so the horizontal part of the operator becomes a multiplication
by $\sigma^2=\sin^2(k_x\Delta x)/\Delta x^2+\sin^2(k_y\Delta y)/\Delta y^2$. If all coefficients were the same throughout each
horizontal layer, every wave $(k_x,k_y)$ would obey its own one-dimensional equation in $z$. The preconditioner makes exactly
this approximation: the coefficients $a_k$, $b_k$ are replaced by their medians over each layer, and the local Lorentz terms of
the horizontal residuals are dropped. For each wave the vertical system is then tridiagonal,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\big(C_k+w_k\bar H_k^2\,\sigma^2+E_k\big)\hat u_k+D_{k-1}\hat u_{k-1}+D_k\hat u_{k+1}=\hat r_k ,\\
C_k=h_{k-1}\bar H_{k-1/2}^2\,b_{k-1}^2+h_k\bar H_{k+1/2}^2\,a_k^2,\qquad D_k=h_k\bar H_{k+1/2}^2\,a_kb_k ,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and is solved in a number of operations proportional to $n$ (Thomas algorithm). The horizontal mean wave ($k_x=k_y=0$) also
carries the anchor term, a rank-one matrix, which is included exactly with the Sherman--Morrison formula on top of the
tridiagonal solve. Applying $\mathsf M^{-1}$ costs one forward two-dimensional Fourier transform per layer, one tridiagonal
solve per wave and one inverse transform. The medians are recomputed at every Gauss--Newton step.

\subsection{Step control}
The solution $\delta u$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:normal} is the step the linear model recommends. Two safeguards protect against the
non-linearity:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cap:} every component is clamped to $|\delta u_{ijk}|\le2$ (the pressure may change by at most a factor
$e^2\approx7.4$ per point and step).
\item \textbf{Backtracking:} try $u+\alpha\,\delta u$ with $\alpha=1,\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\dots$ (at most 12 trials) and accept the
first trial that lowers $\Phi$ and stays inside the equation-of-state table.
\end{enumerate}
If no trial lowers $\Phi$, the iteration stops.

\subsection{Stopping rule}\label{sec:stop}
After each accepted step the relative decrease $\Delta\Phi/\Phi=(\Phi_{\rm old}-\Phi_{\rm new})/\Phi_{\rm old}$ is computed.
The iteration stops when
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Delta\Phi/\Phi<\code{rtol}$ ($10^{-4}$) on \code{n\_consec} = 3 consecutive steps (converged: the objective no longer
decreases appreciably); or
\item the number of steps reaches \code{max\_steps} (100); or
\item no trial step lowers $\Phi$.
\end{itemize}
Requiring several consecutive small decreases avoids stopping on one accidentally small step. The log records which of the
three ended the iteration, together with $\Phi/\Phi_0$, the RMS of $|\R|\bar H$, the number of CG iterations, $\alpha$ and the
number of capped components of every step.

A small relative decrease does not by itself prove that a minimum has been reached. As a diagnostic, one further undamped
Gauss--Newton step is computed at the returned state (but not taken), and its predicted relative decrease of $\Phi$, the
\textbf{Gauss--Newton decrement} $-\delta u^{\top}\mathsf J^{\top}\mathsf W\R/\Phi$, is reported together with the gradient
norm $\|\mathsf J^{\top}\mathsf W\R\|$ and the stop reason. A small decrement means that the linear model sees little left to
gain.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Above $z_{\rm cut}$}{Above z\_cut}}\label{sec:above}
Above the solved region each column is integrated hydrostatically upward from the solved pressure at level $k_c$, with the
trapezoidal rule on the grid levels:
\begin{equation}
u_{k}-u_{k+1}+\frac{g\,(z_k-z_{k+1})}{2}\big(q(T_k,u_k)+q(T_{k+1},u_{k+1})\big)=0,\qquad k=k_c-1,\dots,1 .
\end{equation}
The equation is implicit in $u_k$ and is solved by Newton's method (at most 30 iterations, until $|\Delta u_k|<10^{-12}$). The
result is a column-hydrostatic upper atmosphere whose pressure joins the solved one continuously at $z_{\rm cut}$. If this
continuation leaves the equation-of-state table anywhere, the result is marked invalid.

\section{Optical depth}\label{sec:tau}
With $P$ known everywhere, the table gives $\rho$ and the 500\,nm extinction $\chi_{500}$. The vertical optical depth
\begin{equation}
\tau_{500}(z)=\tau_{\rm top}+\int_z^{z_1}\chi_{500}\,dz'
\end{equation}
is integrated from the top with the trapezoidal rule. The gas above the grid is represented by an exponential continuation of
$\chi_{500}$ with the scale height $H_\chi=(z_1-z_2)/\ln(\chi_2/\chi_1)$ of the top two levels, $\tau_{\rm top}=\chi_1H_\chi$
(half a grid cell, $\tfrac12\chi_1(z_1-z_2)$, if $\chi$ does not decrease upward there or $H_\chi$ exceeds 5000\,km). The height
of $\tau_{500}=1$ is interpolated linearly in $\log_{10}\tau$ between the bracketing levels. Since the opacity grows with
density, any change of pressure moves it.

\section{Validity checks}
The solve is attempted only if every column of the starting state stayed inside the equation-of-state table. The returned
fields are checked at the end: every state inside the table, all values finite, $P$, $\rho$ and $\chi_{500}$ positive, and
$\tau_{500}$ strictly increasing downward in every column. The first failed check, if any, is reported with the number of
points or columns concerned.

\section{Three dimensions: what the result means}
\begin{itemize}
\item Below $z_{\rm cut}$ the pressure balances, as well as a pressure change can, the horizontal and vertical forces of
gravity and the given Lorentz force: mostly their gradient part, and through the density part of the non-gradient part.
It is a minimum of $\Phi$, not an exact equilibrium. The remaining imbalance, the buoyancy of the given temperature
structure that the given field cannot hold up, would require the field (or the temperature) to change.
\item Above $z_{\rm cut}$ the atmosphere is column-hydrostatic, not magnetohydrostatic.
\item The pressure level rests on $P_{\rm ref}$ at the bottom of the starting state and on the anchor penalty on the mean of
the bottom plane.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of the algorithm}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Read $T$, $\B$ and the grid; compute the starting state: column hydrostatic equilibrium with $P=P_{\rm ref}$ at the bottom
of every column.
\item Compute $\J$ and $\Lv=\J\times\B$ once.
\item On the levels $z\le z_{\rm cut}$: compute $\bar H_k$ and the weights; evaluate $\R$ and $\Phi$.
\item Repeat: build the layer-median preconditioner; solve the normal equations by preconditioned CG; clamp the step to
$|\delta u|\le2$; backtrack until $\Phi$ decreases; accept. Stop after three consecutive steps with
$\Delta\Phi/\Phi<10^{-4}$, after 100 steps, or when no step lowers $\Phi$; report the Gauss--Newton decrement at the result.
\item Integrate each column hydrostatically above $z_{\rm cut}$.
\item Compute $\rho$, $\tau_{500}$ and $z(\tau_{500}=1)$; check the fields; write the result.
\end{enumerate}
