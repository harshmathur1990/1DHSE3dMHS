\doctitle{column-hse-z: hydrostatic columns on a height grid}{The physics of \code{run.mode = "column-hse-z"}, explained step by step (B.Sc.\ level). HSEMHS.}

\section{The question}
We are given the temperature $T$ of a piece of the solar atmosphere at every point of a three-dimensional grid: two
horizontal coordinates $x,y$ and the height $z$. We want to know the \textbf{gas pressure} $P$ and the \textbf{density}
$\rho$ at every point, and from them how transparent the gas is, i.e.\ the \textbf{optical depth} $\tau_{500}$ at the
wavelength 500\,nm. The answer this mode gives is the simplest physically consistent one: every vertical column of the grid
is treated as a separate, static column of gas that carries its own weight. Nothing moves, and neighbouring columns do not
interact.

\section{Hydrostatic equilibrium}
Take a thin horizontal slab of gas with area $A$, between heights $z$ and $z+dz$. Three vertical forces act on it:
\begin{itemize}
\item the gas below pushes it up with the force $P(z)\,A$;
\item the gas above pushes it down with $P(z+dz)\,A$;
\item gravity pulls it down with its weight, mass $\times\,g=\rho\,A\,dz\,g$.
\end{itemize}
If the slab does not accelerate, the forces cancel: $P(z)A-P(z+dz)A-\rho gA\,dz=0$. Dividing by $A\,dz$ and letting
$dz\to0$:
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{dP}{dz}=-\rho\,g\;}
\]
The pressure \emph{decreases} upward, and it decreases fastest where the gas is densest. This is \textbf{hydrostatic
equilibrium (HSE)}. It is the same law that makes the air pressure fall as you climb a mountain.

\section{Equation of state: how $\rho$ follows from $T$ and $P$}
\subsection{The ideal gas}
The solar photosphere and chromosphere behave as an ideal gas: $P=n\,k_BT$. If the free particles have an average mass
$\bar\mu\,m_{\rm H}$, the density is $\rho=n\,\bar\mu\,m_{\rm H}$, so
\[
\rho=\frac{\bar\mu\,m_{\rm H}}{k_B}\,\frac{P}{T}.
\]
At fixed temperature, doubling the pressure doubles the density.

\subsection{Why $\bar\mu$ is not a constant}
For neutral solar gas (about 91\,\% hydrogen and 9\,\% helium atoms by number) $\bar\mu\approx1.3$. When hydrogen ionises,
each atom becomes two particles, a proton and an electron, with almost the same total mass, so $\bar\mu$ roughly halves
(to $\approx0.6$ for fully ionised gas). In the coolest layers hydrogen forms H$_2$ molecules, which raises $\bar\mu$. How
much is ionised is described by the \textbf{Saha equation}, for an atom in ionisation stage $i$:
\[
\frac{n_{i+1}\,n_e}{n_i}=\frac{2U_{i+1}}{U_i}\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-E_i/k_BT},
\]
where $E_i$ is the ionisation energy (13.6\,eV for hydrogen), $U$ are partition functions and $h$ is Planck's constant.
Two features matter. The exponential makes ionisation switch on rapidly with temperature. And the factor $n_e$ on the left
means that at higher density (more electrons around to recombine with) a higher temperature is needed for the same
ionisation. So $\bar\mu$ depends on \emph{both} $T$ and $P$.

\subsection{The table}
Solving the Saha equations for all relevant elements together with charge conservation and molecule formation is too slow
to do at every point of every step. It is done once, with Wittmann's equation-of-state code for solar composition, on a
fine grid of $(\ln T,\ln P)$, and stored in a \textbf{table}. The code reads $\ln\rho$, $\ln n_e$ and the 500\,nm
extinction $\ln\chi_{500}$ from it by smooth (bicubic) interpolation. Because all three come from the same solution, they
are consistent with each other.

\section{The scale height and why we work with $\ln P$}
Insert the equation of state into hydrostatic equilibrium:
\[
\frac{dP}{dz}=-\frac{\bar\mu m_{\rm H}g}{k_BT}\,P=-\frac{P}{H},\qquad
H\equiv\frac{P}{\rho g}=\frac{k_BT}{\bar\mu\,m_{\rm H}\,g}.
\]
$H$, the \textbf{pressure scale height}, has the dimension of a length. For $T=6000$\,K and $\bar\mu=1.3$ it is
\[
H=\frac{1.38\times10^{-23}\cdot6000}{1.3\cdot1.67\times10^{-27}\cdot274}\ {\rm m}\approx1.4\times10^5\ {\rm m}=140\ {\rm km}.
\]
If $H$ were constant (an isothermal gas), the solution would be $P(z)=P(z_0)\,e^{-(z-z_0)/H}$: the pressure falls by a
factor $e\approx2.7$ every 140\,km. In general $H$ changes with height, and dividing by $P$ gives the form the code uses:
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{du}{dz}=-\frac{1}{H(T,P)},\qquad u=\ln P\;}
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad u(z)=u(z_0)-\int_{z_0}^{z}\frac{dz'}{H(z')} .
\]
Working with $u=\ln P$ has two advantages. A column spans many factors of ten in pressure, but $u$ changes smoothly and
by moderate amounts, which is what a numerical integration handles best. And $P=e^u$ is automatically positive.

\section{The anchor: one pressure per column}
$du/dz=-1/H$ is a first-order differential equation: it determines how $u$ changes, not its value. Like any such
equation it needs one starting value per column. The mode prescribes the \textbf{same pressure $P_{\rm ref}$ at one level
in every column}, by default the bottom level (\code{anchor = "bottom"}), and integrates upward from there. The other
options are the top level (integrate downward) and a chosen height $z_{\rm ref}$ inside the grid (integrate both ways).

Why the bottom? Deep in the atmosphere the gas is dense and the temperature differences between columns matter least, so
a common pressure there is a reasonable approximation. A top anchor would make the pressure in the photosphere depend
on the very different temperatures of the chromosphere and transition region in each column. Since every column starts
from the same $P_{\rm ref}$, all differences between columns come from their temperatures.

Between grid levels the temperature is not known. The code assumes that $\ln T$ varies linearly with $z$ inside each
interval and integrates with the classical fourth-order Runge--Kutta method, which evaluates the slope $-1/H$ four times
per step and averages them. Each interval is computed with $n$ and $2n$ substeps; if the two disagree by more than
$10^{-6}$ in $u$, the number of substeps is doubled until they agree. Steep temperature changes (the transition region)
thus get more substeps automatically.

\section{Column mass}
Integrating $dP/dz=-\rho g$ from a height $z$ up to the top of the column gives
\[
P(z)-P_{\rm top}=g\int_z^{z_{\rm top}}\rho\,dz'=g\,m(z),
\]
where $m(z)$ is the mass of gas above $z$ per square metre. \emph{The pressure at any point is the weight of the gas
above it} (plus whatever presses on the top). The code integrates $m$ along with $u$ and stores it, which also serves as a
check of the integration.

\section{Light, opacity and optical depth}
\subsection{Extinction and the Beer--Lambert law}
Light of intensity $I$ travelling a distance $ds$ through gas loses a fraction $\chi\,ds$:
\[
dI=-\chi\,I\,ds\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I(s)=I(0)\,e^{-\int_0^s\chi\,ds'}=I(0)\,e^{-\tau}.
\]
The number $\tau=\int\chi\,ds$, the \textbf{optical depth}, counts how many ``absorption lengths'' the light crosses.
$\chi_{500}$ includes both true absorption and scattering at 500\,nm. In the solar photosphere it is dominated by the
negative hydrogen ion H$^-$ (a hydrogen atom with an extra electron, bound by only 0.75\,eV). Forming H$^-$ needs free
electrons, which come mostly from easily ionised metals (Na, Ca, Fe, \dots), so $\chi_{500}$ rises steeply with temperature
and with density. It comes from the same table as $\rho$.

\subsection{Optical depth along the vertical}
For light leaving the Sun vertically, the optical depth of a point at height $z$ is
\[
\tau_{500}(z)=\tau_{\rm top}+\int_z^{z_{\rm top}}\chi_{500}(z')\,dz' .
\]
It grows downward. It would be zero at the very top of the atmosphere; at the top of our box it is $\tau_{\rm top}$,
the optical depth of the gas above the box (see below), which is small but not zero. Light emitted at optical depth $\tau$
escapes with probability $e^{-\tau}$, so most of the continuum light we see comes from around $\tau\approx1$ (the
Eddington--Barbier relation: the emerging intensity is close to the emission at $\tau=1$). The height $z_{\tau1}(x,y)$
where $\tau_{500}=1$ is therefore called the ``visible surface''. Spectral lines are formed over a \emph{range} of optical
depths, described by their contribution functions, and different lines (and different parts of one line) sample different
ranges; $\tau_{500}$ is the common reference scale on which inversions report them. For a viewing angle with $\mu=\cos\theta$ the path is longer by $1/\mu$, and
$\tau_\mu=\tau/\mu$.

\subsection{The gas above the box}
The grid ends at $z_{\rm top}$, but the atmosphere does not. If $\chi$ continues to fall exponentially above the top,
$\chi(z)=\chi_1e^{-(z-z_1)/H_\chi}$, the missing optical depth is
\[
\tau_{\rm top}=\int_{z_1}^{\infty}\chi_1e^{-(z-z_1)/H_\chi}\,dz=\chi_1H_\chi,\qquad
H_\chi=\frac{z_1-z_2}{\ln(\chi_2/\chi_1)},
\]
with $H_\chi$ taken from the top two levels (index 1 is the top). If $\chi$ does not decrease upward there, this makes no
sense, and half a grid cell is used instead, $\tau_{\rm top}=\tfrac12\chi_1(z_1-z_2)$.

\section{What the answer means, and what it does not}
Each column is now in exact vertical equilibrium. But consider two neighbouring columns at the same height, one hot and
one cool. Their pressures differ, so there is a horizontal pressure gradient $\partial P/\partial x\ne0$: a sideways force
that nothing balances. The atmosphere is \textbf{not} in three-dimensional equilibrium. The mode reports this horizontal
force as a diagnostic and labels its output \emph{vertical HSE only}.

Could any choice of pressure make a non-magnetic atmosphere fully static? The full force balance is
$-\nabla P-\rho g\zh=0$. Take the curl of both sides. The curl of a gradient is always zero, and
$\nabla\times(\rho g\zh)=g\,(\partial_y\rho,\,-\partial_x\rho,\,0)$, so
\[
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y}=0 .
\]
A static atmosphere without magnetic field must have the same density everywhere on each horizontal plane, and then the
horizontal equations give $\partial_xP=\partial_yP=0$ too. A real, structured atmosphere (hot granules next to cool lanes)
cannot be static without help: in the Sun that help is the motion of the gas, and in a static model it can only be the
magnetic field. That is the subject of the \code{mhs-*} and \code{full-mhs-z} modes, which use this mode's result as their
starting point.
